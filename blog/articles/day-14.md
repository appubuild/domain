# How to Research Domain Sales: Comparable Sales, Market Evidence and Pricing Mistakes

Domain-sale research can help you understand what buyers have paid in particular transactions. It cannot tell you exactly what your domain will sell for. Sale databases may contain incomplete records, private transactions, reporting errors, or names that are not truly comparable. A disciplined researcher records the source and limitations rather than presenting a selected sale as proof.

> **Quick answer:** separate asking prices from completed sales, verify the source, compare names with similar buyer use and extension, and explain differences. Use a range or qualitative conclusion when evidence is limited; do not claim a guaranteed appraisal.

## What counts as a useful comparable?

A useful comparable is a completed transaction with enough detail to understand why it resembles the domain you are evaluating. Similarity can include the extension, word structure, meaning, buyer type, length, language, timing, and sale venue. No single attribute makes two names comparable.

Record the exact domain, reported amount and currency, sale date, source, transaction status, venue, and any known context. Ask whether the figure includes a marketplace fee, represents a package, or is only an advertised ask. If information is missing, mark it unknown instead of guessing.

## Asking price is not sale price

A public listing shows what a seller hopes to receive. A completed transaction reflects one buyer and seller agreeing under specific conditions. A broker’s claimed sale, a social post, a marketplace listing, and an independently verified transaction carry different evidentiary weight. Keep the categories separate in your notes.

A high asking price may never attract an offer. A lower sale may involve a strategic buyer, urgent need, bundle, or private arrangement. These factors may not repeat. An average of unrelated prices can create false precision, especially when the sample is small or includes outliers.

## A repeatable research workflow

1. **Define the subject.** Write down the exact domain, extension, language, and intended buyer use.
2. **Search for close examples.** Use transparent sale databases or marketplace reports and record how each result was obtained.
3. **Check the transaction type.** Separate completed public reports from listings, broker claims, and estimates.
4. **Compare context.** Note differences in extension, word count, category, timing, and buyer motivation.
5. **Look for missing data.** Check whether the currency, sale date, venue, and verification status are clear.
6. **Build a small evidence table.** Include both relevant and contrary evidence, not only examples that support a desired price.
7. **Write a conclusion with uncertainty.** State what the comparables suggest and what they cannot establish.

| Field | Research note |
|---|---|
| Domain being evaluated | Exact name and extension |
| Comparable | Exact reported name; do not hide important differences |
| Evidence source | Database, marketplace report, or direct verified record |
| Sale status | Completed, asking, claimed, or unknown |
| Price details | Amount, currency, date, gross/net if known |
| Similarities | Buyer use, language, structure, extension |
| Differences | Timing, venue, package, audience, known context |
| Confidence | High/medium/low with a reason, not a fabricated score |

## Avoid these pricing mistakes

- **Cherry-picking a high sale.** Include the closest evidence, even when it suggests a lower range or no conclusion.
- **Treating the listing price as a sale.** Label it clearly as an ask.
- **Comparing different extensions without explanation.** Extension and policy can affect buyer behavior and carrying costs.
- **Ignoring the date.** Market conditions and buyer needs change; an old sale may be less informative.
- **Using a broad category as proof.** A popular industry does not prove demand for every name in that industry.
- **Confusing gross proceeds with net proceeds.** Marketplace fees, payment costs, and taxes may reduce the seller’s amount.
- **Claiming a sale is verified without access to evidence.** Use “reported” when you have only a report.
- **Publishing personal or confidential transaction details.** Respect privacy and contractual restrictions.

## From evidence to a price decision

Use evidence to set a range that matches your goals and costs. A seller who wants a quick exit may choose a different price from a seller who can hold the registration longer. Neither choice is inherently correct. Write down the minimum acceptable net proceeds after fees and renewal costs, then decide when you will revisit the asking price.

If the data are sparse, do not force a number. You can conclude that a domain is difficult to price because there are few close transactions or because the likely buyer is unknown. Explain what additional evidence would change your view, such as a real inquiry or a better-matched completed sale.

For portfolios, define one consistent method, note exceptions, and retain the data source and retrieval date. If you compare prices over time, avoid mixing currencies or nominal amounts without disclosing your method. Consult a tax professional for the reporting of actual sales in your jurisdiction.

### Frequently asked questions

**Is a sales database accurate?** It may be useful, but review its sourcing, definitions, omissions, and correction process. No single database captures every private transaction.

**Can I use a marketplace listing as a comparable?** You can use it as evidence of a seller’s asking price, not as a completed sale.

**Should I average comparable sales?** Only if the sample is genuinely comparable and the method is explained. Often a range and qualitative discussion are more honest.

**How many comparables are enough?** There is no universal count. Relevance and verification matter more than quantity.

**Can I advertise my domain as worth the highest similar sale?** That can mislead buyers if key differences are omitted. Describe evidence accurately and do not imply a guaranteed value.

Use transparent sources and keep an audit trail. This article is educational, not an appraisal, investment recommendation, or promise of sale.
