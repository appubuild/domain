How to Start a Domain Business: Business Models, Startup Costs and a 90-Day Plan

October 23, 2026DomainCraft Editorial4 minute read
Business plan90-day plan

A “domain business” can mean several different activities: investing in registrations, brokering transactions, offering naming research, running a marketplace, selling registration services, or building tools and education. These models have different capital needs, legal duties, customer expectations, and operating risks. Start by choosing a specific customer problem—not by buying a large portfolio.

Quick answer: define the business model, target customer, and service promise; estimate startup and recurring costs using current local quotes; validate demand with small experiments; and set a 90-day plan with stop criteria. No model guarantees revenue or profit.

Choose a model

Model What the business does Main responsibilities
Domain portfolio/investing Holds names for future use or sale Acquisition discipline, renewals, legal screening, sales and liquidity risk.
Brokerage Helps buyers and sellers negotiate Representation terms, conflicts, privacy, transaction workflow, and fees.
Naming/research service Helps clients generate or evaluate names Clear scope, methodology, client confidentiality, and legal-advice boundaries.
Marketplace or sales platform Connects domain listings and buyers Payments, identity, disputes, security, consumer terms, and support.
Registration/reseller service Facilitates registrations through an upstream provider Partner agreements, billing, support, renewal notices, and accurate ownership handling.
Tools and education Publishes guides or provides naming/lookup utilities Accuracy, data limitations, privacy, accessibility, and maintenance.

Some models are regulated or require contracts with registrars, payment processors, or other providers. Confirm what you are legally permitted to offer in each market. Do not imply that a naming tool checks trademarks or availability unless it actually does so and explains its source and limitations.

Validate a customer problem

Interview potential customers about how they currently choose, buy, or manage domains. Ask what they do, where they get stuck, what they pay for today, and what evidence would make them trust a new service. Avoid asking only whether they “like the idea.” A polite yes is not proof of demand.

Build the smallest safe test: a sample research report, a manual naming workshop, a landing page, or a limited tool that uses transparent data. Explain what is automated, what is manual, and what the result cannot establish. Do not collect more personal or business information than needed.

Estimate costs without inventing a universal budget

Startup costs depend on country, business model, staffing, software, legal structure, and scale. Create a spreadsheet with one-time and recurring lines:

  • Domain registrations and renewals, if relevant.
  • Hosting, storage, email, monitoring, and backups.
  • Software subscriptions, data sources, and payment processing.
  • Legal/accounting advice, contracts, and business registration.
  • Marketing, research, support, accessibility, and security work.
  • Marketplace, broker, or reseller partner fees.
  • Taxes and currency/payment charges.

Use current vendor quotes and local professional advice. Mark uncertain figures as assumptions, add a contingency, and calculate the amount you can afford if revenue is zero. Do not treat domain resale proceeds as guaranteed cash flow.

Operating foundations

Define who owns customer data, who can access registrar accounts, how payments are handled, and how disputes are escalated. Publish clear terms, privacy information, refund rules, and contact details. Use multi-factor authentication and least-privilege permissions. Keep an incident plan and backups appropriate to the service.

For a brokerage or marketplace, design payment and transfer processes before accepting money. For a content or tool site, disclose data sources, query limits, and whether results are live. For a portfolio business, keep renewal records and a legal-review process.

A practical 90-day roadmap

Days 1–30: define and validate

Select one customer segment and model. Interview a small set of potential customers. Map competitors and existing workflows. Write a one-page service promise and a cost model using real quotes. Identify legal, privacy, and security questions to take to professionals. Stop if the main customer problem is not clear.

Days 31–60: build a narrow pilot

Create the smallest useful offering. Test it with a limited audience under clear terms. Track completion, support questions, trust concerns, and repeat interest. Do not collect payment or sensitive data until the relevant process is ready. Fix confusing promises and technical limitations first.

Days 61–90: decide whether to continue

Review the evidence: what users completed, what they misunderstood, how much support was needed, what the service cost to operate, and whether customers returned or paid under clear terms. Choose to continue, narrow, change, or stop. Set a budget and risk limit for the next period rather than expanding automatically.

Metrics that help

Track measures tied to the business model: completed registrations, qualified inquiries, successful transfers, tool task completion, customer support resolution, renewal retention, or verified revenue after fees. Define each metric and its data source. Avoid vanity measures such as raw page views when they do not answer whether customers are served.

Frequently asked questions

How much money do I need to start? There is no universal amount. It depends on the model, jurisdiction, staffing, and product. Build a local cost sheet and test with a risk-limited budget.

Can I start by buying domains and waiting? You can, but it is speculative and creates ongoing renewals. A portfolio requires a thesis, legal screening, and an exit plan.

Do I need a license? It depends on what services you provide, where you operate, and whether you handle transactions or customer funds. Consult local professionals and provider agreements.

Can a domain generator be a business by itself? It can be part of a service, but users need accurate limits, useful output, privacy, and a sustainable operating model.

When should I hire a lawyer or accountant? Before material transactions, regulated services, cross-border operations, or uncertain legal/tax obligations—not only after a dispute.

This is a planning guide, not business, tax, legal, or investment advice. Verify current local rules, prices, and partner terms before launch.
